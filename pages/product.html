Product Management

Owning the "what" and "why" of a product feature — working across design, engineering, and the business to decide what gets built.

Day to day

  • Talking to users or customers to understand a problem before proposing a solution
  • Writing product requirements docs (PRDs) that engineers and designers can build from
  • Prioritizing a backlog of ideas against limited engineering time
  • Reviewing usage data and metrics to see whether a shipped feature is working
  • Running or attending standups, design reviews, and sprint planning without owning the code itself

Technical skills & tools

Prioritization frameworks (RICE, MoSCoW) Basic SQL / analytics literacy Writing PRDs & user stories Wireframing basics (Figma) A/B testing fundamentals Cross-functional communication Roadmapping tools (Jira, Productboard)

What an entry-level candidate is expected to know

  • Can reason about trade-offs with limited data — "we don't have enough info, so here's the smallest way to test this" is a strong answer
  • Basic data literacy: can read a dashboard and know what metric actually matters for a given decision
  • Understands enough of the technical side (how APIs work, what "technical debt" means) to have a credible conversation with engineers
  • Associate Product Manager (APM) programs are the most common entry point and often value structured problem-solving over prior PM experience

What makes a strong candidate

  • Asks "what problem are we actually solving?" before jumping to feature ideas
  • Can defend a prioritization decision with a clear framework rather than gut feel
  • Shows they can influence without formal authority — getting an engineer or designer on board through reasoning, not title
  • Has some evidence of building or shipping something, even informally (a class project people used, a club process they redesigned)
